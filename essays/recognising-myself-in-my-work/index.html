Recognizing Myself in My Work

Vadim Vladymtsev8 October 20264 min read

Updated 3 November 2026

I want to recognize my own choices in my work and understand why I made them. That matters in engineering and in projects I pursue out of personal interest. When the connection survives, I want to return to the result even after the task is finished.

Once, I was watching a video essay I had made for myself and enjoying it again. Some time had passed, but I still wanted to open it. I could recognize my choices in the music and in what I had brought together. The work had preserved an idea I could return to and feel again.

In professional projects, that feeling can disappear behind requirements and deadlines. There is a list of things to deliver, feedback to address, and a date to meet. That structure helps people work together. Completed items, though, say little about which decisions I made deliberately or what I can identify as my contribution.

For me, authorship begins in the small places where a choice had to be made. How should the system explain a user's next step? What should happen when that person makes a mistake? Will another developer understand why a component works the way it does? The answers give the work its character. An unusual interface or a technical invention is not a prerequisite.

Consider a simple form. It can reject invalid information and leave the person to work out what happened. It can also explain the problem while preserving what they have already entered. In the second case, someone has thought ahead about another person's mistake. I appreciate decisions like that. They reveal how the creator imagines the person on the other side of the screen.

Personal taste also has to hold up in use. A feature I like may confuse the people using the product. The team may have a different view, and the deadline still matters. Sometimes the thoughtful choice is to let a favorite detail go. Explaining my intention and being willing to hear an objection are both part of authorship for me.

I also need room for projects where I choose the question myself. One is Belka, a research project on a language model for Belarusian that I am developing and testing. I treat it as a research hobby. That lets me work honestly on a question that interests me without having to promise that every experiment will become a large business.

A hobby of this kind still involves real work. There are things I do not understand yet, assumptions to examine, and unsuccessful attempts to revisit. Personal interest gives that effort a reason. I want to understand the problem and find out what I can do with it.

Every working day will not feel like a favorite personal project. Any sustained effort includes obligations that simply have to be fulfilled. I want to understand why I agreed to do that work and which decisions I am prepared to stand behind. That helps me remain connected to the result through the less interesting parts as well.

I also want the people behind a piece of work to be known. Recognition feels good, and I can acknowledge that desire directly. In collaborative work, however, authorship is shared. An account of something a team built should preserve the contributions of its members. Putting my name on the story cannot explain work that other people did.

When I return to an older project, I may see choices I would make differently today. That does not remove the pleasure of revisiting it. The difference between my earlier choice and my current view can show me what I have learned. I want the original interest that made me begin to remain recognizable too.

I want to make things that are useful to others and show the care invested in them. Recognizing myself in a project means remembering what I chose to pay attention to and being willing to explain that choice. That gives me a reason to return to the work after it is finished.
